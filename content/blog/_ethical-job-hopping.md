# Job hopping is not betrayal

> These are my personal views. The compensation examples are general and do not describe any specific current or former employer.

Why is protecting profit called good business when a company does it, but disloyalty when a worker protects their salary?

I am a big proponent of job hopping. Not reckless hopping. Not taking a pile of money and equity from a startup, then leaving before doing any meaningful work. I mean doing the job, asking for better conditions, and moving when the answer is no.

The hop can happen after five years. It can also happen after six months. The calendar does not make the decision ethical. Your behavior does.

The safe-sounding advice is to wait for the next review cycle or for a raise that someone will spontaneously decide to give you.

Good luck with that.

Ask clearly. If the answer is no, believe it and move accordingly.

## Six months is not a moral category

People get nervous when the hop happens six or twelve months after joining. Apparently, once a company hires you, an invisible loyalty clock starts ticking.

I do not buy it.

A company takes a business risk every time it hires someone. Training is part of that risk, not a debt the employee must repay with an arbitrary number of years. If training is the only reason someone should stay, the company needs a better retention strategy.

Leaving can inconvenience a team. That part is real. Document what you own, hand it over, and respect the notice in your contract.

Do not stay underpaid just to protect your employer from turnover.

## Ask first, then act

First, ask. Be specific about what would make you stay. Better pay, more flexibility, a different position, or a real internal career path. Give the company a fair chance to keep you.

If the answer is no or postponed until some future review cycle, do not treat "maybe later" as a plan. Start looking and move accordingly.

When you find the right alternative, resign. Give the notice you agreed to give. Do a proper handover. Leave like a professional.

Job hopping is not magic. You still need skills another employer will pay for. The next offer must also beat what you would leave behind.

The point is not to quit for sport. The point is to stop treating waiting as a career strategy.

That is not betrayal. It is the contract working as written.

## A raise below inflation is a pay cut

If a big company celebrates record-breaking earnings, then gives employees a yearly increase that does not match inflation, that is not a raise in real terms.

[Inflation reduces purchasing power](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/mopo/strategy/html/index.en.html). Yours went down.

The company had a record year. You got a pay cut with a congratulations email.

That shit is ridiculous.

If the market values your work more than your employer does, nobody should be surprised when you take the market's offer.

## Big tech has a different clock

Big tech can add stock to the decision, and stock has its own clock.

Take one possible four-year grant. Suppose 25% vests after the first year, followed by 6.25% every quarter. By the end of year two, half of the grant has vested.

For me, the period between the first and second anniversaries is a useful time to test the market. You have already earned a meaningful part of the grant. The unvested stock and any additional grants give you a concrete number to bring into the next negotiation.

A new employer can decide whether to compensate you for some of what you would leave behind.

After four years, the initial grant is fully vested. There is nothing left to vest. If the refreshers are weak, that original grant is no longer a reason to stay.

This is not a universal formula. Your math changes with the stock price, refresher grants, and the actual vesting schedule. Read your grant documents and calculate the cost of leaving.

Recalculate instead of assuming that staying longer is automatically the responsible choice.

## My career is not a dataset

Look at [my own journey in tech](https://www.mbianchi.dev/customers/). Job hopping paid off for me.

That is one data point, and a survivor-biased one. You are reading the story of someone for whom the strategy worked. It would be dishonest to turn my outcome into a guarantee.

Still, I am not the only person in my circle who has seen the same result.

I have watched it happen in Italy. Thankfully, Italy is not representative of every job market. I have also seen it in the Netherlands, elsewhere in Europe, and in the US.

These are different labor markets. The anecdotal pattern remained the same. Moving created opportunities that waiting did not.

This does not prove that job hopping always pays. It does make "job hopping never pays" hard to take seriously.

## The one boundary I care about

Very little about changing jobs is unethical by itself. My main exception is a startup.

If you accept a lot of money and equity, then leave before doing any meaningful work, I consider that unethical. A startup makes a concentrated bet on each hire. One person can own an important part of the company while consuming cash the company cannot casually replace.

That still does not create a debt measured in years.

The standard is impact. Do the work. Create some value. Then move if the job no longer works for you.

For me, the ethical concern is smaller at a company large enough to absorb one departure. Even there, the basic rule remains the same. Do the job and leave cleanly.

## Turnover is feedback

Companies optimize for profit. Nobody expects them to keep an expensive supplier out of loyalty. Workers should be allowed to optimize their side of the contract too.

If people keep leaving, the useful question is not, "What is wrong with this generation?" It is, "What does staying here offer them?"

High turnover should force a company to examine what it pays and whether people can build a career there. Benefits, flexibility, and management matter too.

Training people and then expecting gratitude to retain them is not a strategy.

Make staying competitive.

Some people will leave anyway. Fine. But if departures become a pattern, blaming job hopping only hides the message employees are already sending.

Companies are allowed to protect their profit. Workers are allowed to protect their pay and working conditions.

The ethical test is not how many months appear beside a company name on your CV. It is whether you did the work and left according to the agreement you signed.

When you ask for better conditions and the company says no, it has made a business decision. When you accept a better offer, you are making yours.

If protecting profit is not betrayal, protecting your salary is not betrayal either.

# Ethical Job Hopping

I am a big proponent of job hopping, and whatever people say about it, it can be done ethically.

How?  
You go to your current employer and ask for an improvement in your financial conditions or your position in general.  

99 percent will not be given to you, so at that point you move accordingly to change.  

When you have found the right alternative, you communicate your resignation to your employer with the contractually required notice.  

Do you do this 6 to 12 months after joining the company?  

It doesn't matter when, the important thing is that you have the skills to “hopping” to the next job, that if you wait for a salary review or a spontaneously given raise you are ruined!
  
Job hopping is as ethically correct as it is for a company to think about its profit.  
Why do companies do and workers don't?

Then, if the company has any foresight at all, over time it will understand that if it has high turnover it will have to work to hold on to employees through a system of salary adjustment/revision, benefits, and internal career path.
