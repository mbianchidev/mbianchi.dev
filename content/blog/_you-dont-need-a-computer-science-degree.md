# You don't need a computer science degree. You do need to study.

I started working in tech when I was 15 or 16. I built WordPress websites and tried to sell them to small local businesses.

It kind of worked.

So I kept going. I learned full-stack development without a mentor. During high school, I interned at different companies. Before I graduated, I was already working as a founding engineer.

Full stack, zero mentors. It sounds better than it felt. I learned a lot, but I also spent a stupid amount of time discovering basic lessons on my own.

This is why I do not believe a computer science degree is required to build a serious career in software.

That does not mean university is useless. People studying computer science are not wasting their time. A good degree can give you structure, professors, peers, internships, and exposure to subjects that daily work may never force you to study properly.

The silly part is pretending it is the only legitimate way in.

A company once rejected my direct application because I did not have a degree. A few weeks later, the same company hired me as a consultant for roughly the same pay, on the same team, doing the same work.

Apparently I was unqualified as an employee but perfectly qualified as an invoice.

Nothing about my ability changed. Only the contract did. The degree requirement had not tested whether I could do the job. It had filtered my CV.

Credentials still matter. Without a degree, some recruiters will reject you before a technical conversation starts. Some jobs and immigration systems care a lot about formal education. You may have to prove yourself twice before somebody lets you prove yourself once.

But even the biggest companies accept another route. At the time of writing, a [Google early-career software engineering role](https://www.google.com/about/careers/applications/jobs/results/78703249065943750) lists a bachelor's degree or equivalent practical experience among its minimum qualifications.

That does not make Google easy to get into without a degree. It proves that a degree is one way to show competence, not the only one.

If Google can understand that, the average software company probably can too.

What makes software unusual is how available practice is.

People often compare it with medicine, chemistry, or civil engineering. The point is not that those subjects cannot be studied at home. They can. The difference is the distance between learning something and testing it in the real world.

With a computer and an internet connection, you can learn a language, build something, and put it in front of users. Then reality gives you the rude part of the lesson. The software works or it does not. A client pays you or they do not. You fix it and try again.

This does not make software easy. It makes practice available.

It also lets you build evidence before anyone gives you a credential. A project with real users proves something a transcript cannot. A transcript proves something a weekend project cannot. They are different evidence.

Self-teaching is not the absence of study. It moves responsibility for the curriculum onto you.

You have to choose what to learn, find your own gaps, and keep raising the difficulty. The hardest part becomes obvious only later. You do not know what you do not know.

A degree can introduce you to algorithms, data structures, operating systems, networking, databases, programming languages, and the mathematics beneath parts of the field. You can learn all of that independently. First, though, you need to know it exists and why it matters.

The industry's tools also move faster than most degree programs. That does not make the fundamentals obsolete. It makes the degree insufficient on its own.

Nobody graduates finished.

Since then, I have crossed paths with many engineers. Many had degrees, though not always in computer science or software engineering. Others had none. I cannot remember one saying that the specific diploma was what made their career. What mattered afterward was whether they kept learning and could do the work.

That is still my experience, not a universal law. A degree can open doors. In some specialties, it may be the best route. For some people, the structure and relationships alone justify university.

None of that justifies blocking someone from a career because they learned by working.

University is a path. It is not a permission slip.

You can build a career in software without a computer science degree. You cannot build one without studying and proving that you can do the work. Once you are in, you cannot stop learning just because somebody handed you a diploma.

The degree is optional. The work is not.
