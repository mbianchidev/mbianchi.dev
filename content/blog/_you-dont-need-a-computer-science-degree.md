# You don't need a degree. You need to study.

A (CS) degree can open doors, but it is not the only legitimate way into tech. I discussed this topic many times with my colleagues, peers and friends. Spoiler: most of them have degrees, a good chunk of them regrets not having started to work like I did, even if they had quite some fun in University. I do regret not trying harder to get into one (while working).

I started working in tech when I was 16. I built WordPress websites and sold them to small local businesses in my small town.

It kind of worked. So I kept going. I learned full-stack development without a mentor. During high school, I interned at different companies. Before I graduated, I was already working as a founding engineer for a startup.

Full stack, zero mentors. It sounds better than it felt. I learned a lot, but I also spent a stupid amount of time discovering basic lessons on my own. I still spend that same stupid amount of time keeping myself updated. This industry never stops and never slowed down so far.

Here I am. More than 12 years later, still around IT, no degree.

My story is a big part of why I do not believe a computer science degree is required to build a serious career in software. Call it a statistically irrelevant survivorship bias, but I do not think it is.

That does not mean university is useless. People studying computer science or other degrees are not wasting their time. A good degree can give you structure, professors, peers, internships, and exposure to subjects that daily work may never force you to study and understand properly - especially the foundations and the fundamentals.

The silly part is pretending having a (CS/Engineering) degree is the only legitimate way in. Or believing that holding a degree makes a person more suitable for a role.

A company once rejected my direct application because I did not have a degree. A few weeks later, the same company hired me as a consultant for roughly the same pay, on the same team, doing the same work. With an additional middle man company profiting off the extra consulting margin. Good job folks.

Apparently I was unqualified as an employee but perfectly qualified as an invoice.

Nothing about my ability changed. Only the contract did. The degree requirement had not tested whether I could do the job. It had filtered me by my education CV.

Credentials still matter. Without a degree, some recruiters will reject you before a technical conversation starts. Some jobs and immigration systems in certain countries care a lot about formal education. You may have to prove yourself twice before somebody lets you prove yourself once.

But even the biggest companies accept another route. At the time of writing, a [Google early-career software engineering role](https://www.google.com/about/careers/applications/jobs/results/78703249065943750) lists a bachelor's degree or equivalent practical experience among its minimum qualifications.

That does not make Google easy to get into without a degree. It proves that a degree is one way to show competence, not the only one.

If Google can understand that, the average software company probably can too.

What makes software unusual is how available practice is.

People often compare software engineering with medicine, chemistry, or proper engineering (civil, mechanical, etc.). The point is not that those subjects cannot be studied at home. They can. The difference is the distance between learning something and testing it in the real world.

With a computer and an internet connection, you can learn a programming language, build something, and put it in front of users. Then reality gives you the rude part of the lesson. The software works or it does not. A client pays you or they do not. You fix it and try again.

This does not make software easy. It makes practicing more available.

It also lets you build evidence before anyone gives you a credential. A project with real users proves something a transcript cannot. A transcript proves something a weekend project cannot. They are different evidence.

Self-teaching is not the absence of study. It moves responsibility for the curriculum onto oneself.

You have to choose your own "why", what to learn, find your own knowledge gaps, and keep raising the difficulty in a sustainable way. The hardest part becomes obvious only later. You do not know what you do not know.

A degree can introduce you to algorithms, data structures, operating systems, networking, databases, programming languages, and the mathematics beneath parts of the field. You can learn all of that independently. First, though, you need to know it exists and why it matters.

The industry's tools also move faster than most degree programs. That does not make the fundamentals obsolete. It makes a degree insufficient on its own.

Nobody graduates as a software engineer, that requires actual experience.

Even more in this AI liminal space we live in. The basics are still very important, most people today are not able to solve an equation on paper (they also do not need to) and tomorrow they won't be able to write a for loop without a coding agent.

The past is full of current generations calling the next one "stupid", the future won't be that different. I'd love to be proven wrong, though.

Anyway, all of this to say: I have crossed paths with many engineers. Many had degrees, though not always in computer science or software engineering.

Others had none. I cannot remember one saying that the specific diploma was what made their career. What mattered afterward was whether they kept learning and could do the work.

That is still my experience, not a universal law. A degree can open doors. In some specialties, it may be the best route. For some people, the structure and relationships alone justify enrolling in university.

None of that justifies blocking someone from a career because they learned by doing.

University is a path. It should not be seen as a permission slip.

You can build a career in software without a computer science degree. You cannot build one without studying and proving that you can do the work. Once you are in, you cannot stop learning just because somebody handed you a diploma.

The degree is optional. The work is not.

Just do the work.
